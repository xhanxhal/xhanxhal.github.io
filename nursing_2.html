The Dark Side
of Nursing.

Nursing is often described through compassion, care and responsibility. But behind the uniform is a job that can involve workplace violence, sexual harassment, night-shift insecurity, intimate clinical care, burnout, bullying, physical exposure and repeated contact with illness and death.

This is not an attack on nurses. It is an evidence-based look at the difficult realities of the profession — the risks that are easy to overlook when we only see the caring side of nursing.

02 / Workplace violence

The workplace itself can become a source of danger.

In a 2021 Bangladesh study of 1,264 registered nurses, 70% reported at least one form of workplace violence during the previous year.

22.2%

Physical assault reported by nurses in the study.

65.5%

Emotional abuse was reported — the largest category in the study.

39.2%

Threats were reported by participating nurses.

14.8%

Verbal sexual harassment was reported.

5.9%

Sexual abuse was reported in this nurse-specific study.

Reality

A clinical workplace is not automatically a violence-free workplace.

Methodology note: The 2021 nurse study used self-reported data and convenience sampling. It should not be interpreted as the exact national rate for every nurse in Bangladesh.
03 / Relatives & visitors

Sometimes the pressure comes from outside the ward.

Patient relatives and visitors can become sources of verbal aggression, threats and, in some cases, physical violence — particularly when a patient is critically ill or family members are frustrated.

Angry confrontation — complaints can escalate quickly.
Threats — nurses may be personally targeted after a bad outcome.
Physical aggression — conflict can move beyond words.
Emotional pressure — repeated exposure can contribute to stress.

2022–23 Bangladesh study

A nationwide study of 3,977 healthcare professionals found 77.29% reported workplace violence.

Among reported perpetrators, patient relatives/friends accounted for 43.55% and visitors for 31.38%.

Healthcare professionals — not nurses only
04 / Intimate patient care

The most private part of the job.

Some clinical procedures require a nurse to work around a patient's most private body areas. This can be one of the most uncomfortable realities to understand from outside the profession.

Urinary catheterization

During catheterization, the required part of the genital area may need to be exposed and positioned. A nurse may need to see or physically position/hold the area as necessary to safely perform the clinical procedure.

Professional - sexual

Seeing or handling a patient's private area for a medical procedure is clinical care, nurses can feel uneasy. Also, the negative issue begins when a patient crosses professional boundaries or sexualizes the situation.

The uncomfortable truth:
A nurse can be required to provide intimate care while still being expected to maintain complete professionalism, privacy and emotional composure.
Intimate care can be uncomfortable or emotionally stressful for some nurses, but it would be inaccurate to claim that intimate procedures automatically cause psychological harm. The greater concern is when clinical intimacy is combined with harassment, lack of privacy, poor support or unsafe workplace conditions.
05 / Sexual harassment

Professional boundaries can be tested.

Inappropriate comments

Sexual jokes, comments about appearance or suggestive conversation can create an unsafe environment.

Unwanted touching

Clinical proximity does not give a patient permission to cross personal boundaries.

Deliberate exposure

Intentional inappropriate behaviour can turn a routine clinical situation into harassment.

Stalking / attention

Repeated unwanted attention can continue beyond a single interaction.

Sexual assault

More serious physical violations can occur and should never be treated as part of the job.

Underreporting

Fear of consequences, stigma or lack of trust in reporting systems can discourage complaints.

06 / Night shift

Night duty changes the equation.

Night shift can mean disrupted sleep, fatigue, long working hours and additional security concerns. A 2025 qualitative Bangladesh study reported concerns from nurses about night duty, security and sexual harassment.

Sleep disruption — normal sleep patterns can be repeatedly interrupted.
Fatigue — long shifts can leave little recovery time.
Security concerns — particularly relevant for female nurses.
Travel risk — the workday may begin or end at unusual hours.

Night shift in the data

In a 2022–23 nationwide healthcare-worker study, night shift was associated with a workplace-violence prevalence of 35.66%.

07 / Mental pressure

When the job follows you home.

Nursing can involve repeated exposure to suffering, death, conflict, bullying and high responsibility. The problem is not simply being “tired”; sustained occupational stress can become a serious mental-health burden.

54.19%BurnoutBangladeshi registered nurses · 2021 study
61.79%BullyingWorkplace bullying reported in the same nurse cohort
13.26%High suicidal ideationReported in a study of the same broader nurse cohort
—Not a simple causeAssociations do not prove that one workplace factor caused suicidal ideation.
Mental-health statistics must be interpreted carefully. Research can identify associations between bullying, violence, burnout and psychological outcomes without proving that any single factor caused an individual person's mental-health condition.
08 / The workload

High responsibility. Sometimes inadequate support.

Long shifts

Extended duty periods can create physical and cognitive fatigue.

Staff shortages

Fewer people carrying more patients increases pressure.

Limited breaks

Qualitative research from Bangladesh has documented concerns about insufficient break time.

Equipment gaps

Some nurses report inadequate equipment for ideal patient management.

Low recognition

Heavy responsibility does not always come with proportional recognition.

Emotional labour

Nurses often have to remain calm and reassuring even under personal stress.

10 / Death & emotional trauma

Sometimes there is no time to process what happened.

A nurse can spend hours caring for a critically ill patient, witness a deterioration, support a grieving family and then return to another patient almost immediately. The work does not necessarily stop because an emotionally difficult event has happened.

One patient dies. The family grieves. The room changes.
And then the nurse has to continue the shift.

Repeated exposure to suffering and death can contribute to emotional exhaustion and compassion fatigue. The exact effect varies between individuals and workplaces, but the emotional labour is a real part of nursing.

11 / When reporting fails

The second problem can be what happens after the incident.

Violence becomes harder to control when workers do not trust the reporting and accountability process.

12 / The hardest truth

Who protects the person who protects everyone else?

A nurse may be expected to protect a patient's dignity while working in conditions that do not always protect the nurse's own dignity. That contradiction sits at the centre of nursing's darker side.

Patient dignity

Nurses may provide intimate care while preserving privacy and respect.

Professional composure

They are expected to remain calm even during abuse, grief or crisis.

Personal safety

Clinical responsibility should never mean accepting harassment or violence.

Nursing's dark side is not that nurses are “bad”.
It is that a caring profession can expose the people doing the caring to physical, psychological and social risks that are often invisible from outside.
Research timeline

What the evidence shows over time

2021

1,264 registered nurses: workplace violence, including physical assault, emotional abuse, threats, verbal sexual harassment and sexual abuse, was documented.

2021

Same nurse cohort: research documented workplace bullying and burnout, highlighting the psychological burden of unsafe workplaces.

2022–2023

3,977 healthcare professionals: nationwide research reported a high level of workplace violence and examined perpetrators, shifts and consequences.

2025

Qualitative nursing research: nurses described workload, low recognition, night-duty concerns, security and other workplace challenges.
